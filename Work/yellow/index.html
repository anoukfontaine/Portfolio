Case Study

Yellow is the New Green

Transforming a fragmented, Excel-based financial planning process into an interactive dashboard that helps decision-makers understand data, recognize correlations, and make informed decisions.

Note: To protect internal information, the illustrations and mockups have been simplified. The main dashboard mockup is entirely fictional and is intended only to convey the general type and structure of the solution.

What

A financial dashboard for planning and monitoring a CHF 400+ million annual budget, used by decision-makers, data analysts and operational teams within the Swiss federal administration, cutting manual preparation work by 90% and achieving 100% satisfaction among primary users.

Why

A fragmented budget planning process required extensive manual work, while financial information was difficult for users to navigate, leading to low confidence in the data.

How

End-to-end ownership of both the project and product, from UX/UI design through Power BI development, rollout and long-term maintenance. With no prior Power BI experience, rapidly learning the tool alongside my usual responsibilities was an integral part of the process.

Problem

Important decisions were slowed down by fragmented information.

Time-consuming

Data analysts spent days preparing information before they could actually analyze and discuss it with decision-makers.

Limited exploration

Users could not easily filter, compare or drill into the data without creating additional views and calculations.

Low confidence in the data

Because information was spread across several files, users often questioned whether they were working with the most up-to-date and reliable data.

Solution

All in one.

One Power BI dashboard brings all relevant information together in one place, making it accessible and usable across user groups.

One source

All relevant financial information is brought together in one dashboard instead of being spread across multiple files.

More confidence

Users can filter, compare and drill into the data directly, without creating additional views or performing manual calculations, making the information easier to understand, explain and trust.

Easier to access

A centralized, up-to-date view makes information easier to access and find, while an export function still enables users to carry out their own analysis when needed.

01

Understand

Stepping into the users' shoes.

To better understand how users interacted with financial information, I conducted interviews and mapped user journeys to reveal recurring frustrations, uncover needs and define the main pain points that ultimately shaped the dashboard. Key Decision: I prioritized section heads as the primary users because they drive planning and decision-making, while ensuring the solution also remained useful for other roles.

User journey map

02

Design

Designing for clarity.

Financial data can easily feel dense and intimidating. The visual design therefore played a key role in making complex information approachable. Working within a public-sector context, I used the clean and restrained Swiss federal design system and, where elements were missing, drew inspiration from existing Swiss government dashboards. Key Decision: I organized the dashboard into multiple focused pages with a consistent layout rather than overloading a single page with information.

Using paper cutouts to explore different dashboard structures

Design decisions (hover to discover)

Federal color palette

The color palette draws on the existing federal design system, prioritizing more neutral colors to reduce cognitive load.

Federal typography

Typography follows the federal design guidelines, using Arial as the required typeface. Its familiarity and readability support efficient information scanning.

Filters that leave no one behind

Filters were prioritized for primary users, with the most relevant options placed prominently at the top for quick access, while secondary users could still reach the information they needed through less prominent filters positioned lower on the page and through drill-downs.

Exact figures, when needed

Tables complement the visualizations by providing precise values for users who need exact figures. They can also be exported to Excel, supporting further analysis and reporting.

Choosing the right chart

Rather than relying on a single chart type, each visualization was chosen to present the data as clearly as possible. Consistent color coding, such as slate blue for disbursements and federal red for targets, makes categories easy to recognize across charts and pages, while neutral tones reduce visual clutter.

03

Build

Learning by doing.

Power BI was completely new territory for me. Through online training and hands-on experimentation, I learned the tool while building the solution alongside my regular responsibilities. Introducing a new technology also required close stakeholder communication, change management and new access and governance processes for the dashboard. Key Decision: Whenever technical constraints made an idea unworkable, I returned to the underlying user need and looked for a feasible alternative.

Data model in Power BI

04

Test

Does it actually work?

A moderated usability study with three users identified areas for improvement before launch. Four months later, a voluntary questionnaire was distributed to all primary users to evaluate adoption, usability and satisfaction. The response rate was 83%, and the results confirmed strong acceptance of the new dashboard and way of working. Key Decision: Given the strong level of acceptance, we decided to phase out the old tools and stop maintaining them.

05

Evolve

Built to evolve.

Since launch, the dashboard has served as the division's sole tool for planning and monitoring its annual budget of over CHF 400 million. Not a single user has asked for any of the old Excel files. The dashboard has also continued to evolve: new overviews have addressed additional user needs and made everyday work easier, while enabling analyses that had not previously been possible.
