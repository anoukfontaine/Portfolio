Case Study

Browsing the World's Economy

Transforming static country reports into an interactive public dashboard that makes economic information accessible, searchable and automatically updated.

Note: Due to confidentiality and intellectual property considerations, this case study uses simplified, illustrative visuals. The live dashboard is publicly available and linked below.

View live product

What

A multilingual public Power BI dashboard exploring country-specific economic data for businesses, policymakers, researchers, the general public and specialists within the Swiss federal administration

Why

Replace manually produced Word-based country reports to improve the user experience and reduce preparation time

How

As the sole designer, I supported a cross-functional team of BI engineers and subject matter experts with UX design best practices and data visualization expertise

Problem

Hello Word.

Manual reports

Each country report had to be created and updated manually in Microsoft Word.

Outdated information

Screenshots and copied data increased the risk of outdated or inconsistent information.

Hard to explore

Users could not easily search, compare or interact with the data.

Role

I acted as the bridge between subject matter experts and technical implementation. As the project's sole UX and data visualization designer, I translated complex economic information into clear and accessible data visualizations and helped produce an engaging user experience.

Constraints

A limited budget, a fixed website go-live deadline, delayed access to data, four languages to support and Power BI technical limitations all had to be navigated.

01

Research

Missing the start of the party.

I joined the project six months after it had officially started. Progress had been limited due to delayed access to the data. An initial workshop had taken place at the start of the project, and no further user research was planned.

Challenge

Joining after the initial research and project direction had already been defined, with the goal largely focused on reproducing the existing solution.

Solution

Build on the existing direction while identifying opportunities to improve the user experience and advocate for a more user-centered approach.

02

Design

Cutting Word into pieces.

We revisited the report’s initial structure from scratch. We literally cut the Word document apart and explored how the information could be reorganized into a more intuitive dashboard experience. I also questioned the use of tables and suggested visualizations where I felt they made sense. Reviewing and refining these changes with experts helped us find a structure that was both intuitive for users and accurate from an expert perspective.

Challenge

Ensure the needs of users with limited economic expertise were represented, despite project decisions being driven by domain experts.

Solution

I relied on visualization best practices to make complex economic data more approachable, choosing clearer chart types, simplifying layouts, and using color and visual hierarchy to guide the eye toward key information.

Reorganizing the information architecture

Design decisions (hover to discover)

Color with purpose

The color palette builds on existing federal dashboards to create a familiar and trustworthy experience. Federal red is reserved exclusively for Switzerland, making comparisons immediate and intuitive. Only the country name is highlighted, while numerical values remain neutral to avoid unintended negative associations.

Designing within constraints

Typography followed the federal design guidelines, with Arial as the required typeface. Its familiarity and readability support efficient information scanning. Technical limitations prevented adjustments to letter and line spacing, requiring the design to work within a denser typographic layout.

Guiding the eye

Icons were used sparingly to improve orientation and scanability without competing with the data.

Stay oriented

A persistent sidebar provides clear orientation, while the country filter lets users seamlessly switch between country profiles from any page.

Choosing the right chart

Rather than relying on a single chart type, each visualization was selected for the data it communicates best. Consistent colors, with blue for exports and mint for imports, reinforce recognition across charts, while neutral tones prevent visual clutter.

Organizing complexity

A consistent card-based layout organizes both information and key indicators into clear, easy-to-scan sections. Key figures are immediately visible, while consistent color coding distinguishes export and import metrics. Displaying data sources directly on each card reinforces transparency and trust.

Exact figures, when needed

Tables complement the visualizations by providing precise values for users who need exact figures. They can also be exported to Excel, supporting further analysis and reporting.

03

Iterate

Good things come in threes.

The project evolved through three major phases: developing an MVP, refining it through multiple rounds of feedback and iteration, and preparing the public release through further testing and improvements.

Challenge

Technical limitations frequently reshaped what was possible and required continuous follow-up.

Solution

Continuously review the implementation, prioritize the design elements that had the greatest impact on usability, and come up with alternative solutions when technical limitations occurred.

A feedback document helped track and prioritize design changes

04

Launch

Going public.

The final version was released as a public Power BI dashboard. Instead of manually assembled country reports, users with very different levels of expertise can now explore economic information through an automated, multilingual and interactive experience.

Challenge

The dashboard had to be ready for the website go-live while still meeting public-facing quality standards across four languages.

Solution

Maintain close collaboration with the Power BI englineers by responding quickly, preparing implementation-ready documentation and resolving questions as they arose.

Reflection

Learning to let go.

Before this project, I had mainly worked as both the designer and Power BI engineer, taking ideas from design through to implementation myself. On this project, I had to hand over the implementation and learn to communicate my design intent clearly while remaining open to solutions that differed from what I had initially imagined.

Letting go of implementation control was a learning experience. At the same time, I found that the Power BI engineers appreciated concrete input on how certain ideas could be built, and I could occasionally help uncover possibilities within the tool.
Not every user perspective could be directly represented throughout the process, which made it even more important to rely on accessibility, usability and data visualization best practices when making design decisions.
Working with people from different disciplines, generations and areas of expertise required alignment and compromise, but those different perspectives also challenged assumptions and led to stronger solutions.
