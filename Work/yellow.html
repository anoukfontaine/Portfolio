Case Study

Yellow is the new Green

Transforming a fragmented, Excel-based financial planning process into an interactive dashboard that helps decision-makers understand, explore and trust their data.

Note: To protect internal information, the content of the case study has been anonymized and simplified. The main dashboard mockup is entirely fictional and is intended only to convey the general type and structure of the solution.

What

An interactive financial dashboard supporting the planning and monitoring of an annual budget of over CHF 400 million used by decision-makers, data analysts and operational teams within the Swiss federal administration

Why

Increase clarity, accessibility, and confidence in data while saving time and resources

How

End-to-end project and product ownership, from designing in Figma to implemeting in Power BI

Problem

Important decisions were slowed down by fragmented information.

Time consuming

Data analysts spent days preparing information before they could actually analyze and discuss it with decision-makers.

Limited exploration

Users could not easily filter, compare or drill into the data without creating additional views and calculations.

Limited trust

Because information was spread across several files, users often questioned whether they were working with the latest and most reliable data.

Role

End-to-end ownership of the product, from user research and UX/UI design to Power BI development, rollout and continuous iteration.

Constraints

With no prior Power BI experience, I designed and developed the solution alongside my regular responsibilities, requiring continuous learning and rapid upskilling throughout the project.

01

Research

From assumptions to insights.

To better understand how users interacted with financial information, I conducted interviews and mapped user journeys to reveal recurring frustrations, uncover needs and define the main pain points that ultimately shaped the dashboard.

Challenge

There were several user groups with different needs. Designing for everyone equally risked creating a dashboard that was too generic.

Solution

Map user types, identify primary users and prioritize the needs of the people who drive planning and decision-making, even though they were not the largest user group.

User journey map

02

Design

Designing for clarity.

Financial data can easily feel dense and intimidating. The visual design therefore played a key role in making complex information approachable and supporting quick decision-making. Working within a public-sector context, I opted for a clean and restrained visual language inspired by existing Swiss government dashboards, prioritizing clarity, accessibility and trust over decoration.

Challenge

Making a CHF 400M financial planning process understandable at a glance while preserving the level of detail required for operational decision-making, all within the technical constraints of Power BI.

Solution

Careful use of visual hierarchy, filtering, progressive disclosure, color coding and interactive drill-downs, while continuously validating every design decision against Power BI's technical constraints to ensure that the final experience could be implemented as intended.

Using paper and Figma to generate ideas

Design decisions

Build trust through consistency

Consistent layouts, standardized metrics and a single source of truth help users understand the data faster and increase confidence in the information.

Optimize for high-impact users

Although several user groups used the dashboard, the experience was primarily designed around the needs of the people driving planning and budget decisions. Secondary users could still access the information they needed through filtering and drill-downs.

Leaving no one behind

Specific filters for non-primary user groups were added to ensure that all users could access the information they needed.

Choosing the right graphics

Preserving existing graphics where appropriate, while adding new ones where they added value.

Less color, more clarity

Rather than adopting the full federal color palette, a restrained set of neutral tones was used to create a calm, data-focused interface. Federal red remains the only accent color, drawing attention where it matters without creating unnecessary visual clutter.

Make every pixel count

As the dashboard was intended for internal use, strict adherence to the official design system was not required. A more compact typeface was chosen to accommodate longer texts.

03

Develop

Learning by doing.

Power BI was initially completely new territory for me. Through online training and extensive hands-on experimentation, I successfully delivered the solution while continuing my regular responsibilities. Introducing a new technology also required extensive stakeholder communication, change management and the creation of new access and governance processes around the dashboard. Within a year, I had become a recognized point of contact for dashboard design and Power BI within the organization.

Challenge

Bringing ambitious design ideas to life in an unfamiliar platform while introducing a new technology, defining governance processes and ensuring the final product could fully replace an existing business-critical process.

Solution

Breaking complex features into smaller problems, leveraging expert support and AI whenever technical challenges arose, and ensuring stakeholder buy-in through transparent communication, early involvement and continuous support.

Data model in Power BI

04

Test

Does it actually work?

A moderated usability study with three users uncovered four key improvements before launch. Four months later, a voluntary questionnaire was distributed to all primary users to evaluate adoption, usability and satisfaction. The response rate was 83%, and the results confirmed strong acceptance of the new dashboard and way of working.

Challenge

Because the usability testing was conducted on a functional product, implementing changes required additional development effort and careful prioritization.

Solution

Prioritize the changes based on their impact on usability and the effort required to implement them.

Reflection

Growing with the product.

This project gave me the chance to take a product from the initial concept to a live, continuously evolving dashboard, shaping the UX and visual design, working closely through development, supporting the launch, and continuing to own it after release.

Since then, I’ve expanded the dashboard with new project overviews that bring key information together in one place, so users can spend less time working through the data and more time managing their projects.

I started the project with very limited Power BI experience. Building a real product with a clear purpose made learning the tool much faster than following tutorials in isolation. I could immediately apply what I learned, test it and improve it. AI accelerated this process even further by helping me understand unfamiliar concepts, troubleshoot technical issues and explore different ways of implementing ideas.
Successful product development depends on more than the people who directly use the product. Stakeholders who provide data, maintain systems, approve decisions or support implementation can have a major influence on the pace of a project. Bringing them into the process early, understanding their constraints and building shared ownership can remove blockers and significantly accelerate development.
Iterations that take minutes in Figma can take considerably longer once implemented. This is particularly true for visual and interaction design, where Power BI can be restrictive and even small design changes may require significant effort. Testing structure, navigation and visual concepts in a prototype first therefore makes experimentation much faster and avoids spending development time on ideas that may still change.
